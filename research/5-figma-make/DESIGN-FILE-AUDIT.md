Two sourdough bakery landing pages generated by the Figma agent from the same
prompt in the same file on the same day. One with the published
`/ai-a11y-toolkit` skill invoked, one with no skill invoked.

File: https://www.figma.com/design/K2UUFuil7G3IqbUP0ZP01F/AI-A11y-Toolkit

# Summary

A design file is a static artifact. It determines exactly three WCAG 2.2 AA
criteria, influences fourteen, and cannot affect the remaining thirty-eight. This
study scores the three it determines and records the state of what it influences.
That is the whole available result, and it is deliberately a narrower comparison
than the twenty-check build studies.

| Criterion | What a design file can do | Control | Treatment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1.4.3 Text contrast | Determines | **19 of 47 pairings pass** | **40 of 41 pairings pass** |
| 1.4.11 Non-text contrast | Determines | All component boundaries pass | **Subscribe button at 2.71:1** |
| 2.5.8 Target size, 24 x 24, **Level AA** | Determines | **Pass**, via the spacing exception | **Pass**, via the spacing exception |

**Headline, scored strictly at Level AA: 2 of 3 for the control, 1 of 3 for the
treatment.**

This file has been corrected twice since publication and the second correction was
itself wrong in two places. It first reported 1 of 3 against 3 of 3, then a 1 of 3
tie. Both were wrong, and the current numbers are the third attempt. Every
correction is recorded in [Scoring corrections](#scoring-corrections) below rather
than quietly folded in.

**On the headline count the toolkit arm now scores below the control.** That is the
result and it is reported here at the same length it would get if it ran the other
way. It turns on one button, and the sentence underneath it about magnitude within
1.4.3 is the finding that survives all three scorings intact.

The toolkit's 44 x 44 target requirement is a house standard drawn from
[SC 2.5.5 Target Size (Enhanced)](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#target-size-enhanced),
which is **Level AAA**. It is not the AA criterion, and this file previously scored
it under the 2.5.8 label. Measured against the house standard the separation is
clean, 0 of 6 control buttons against 9 of 9 treatment controls, and that result is
reported in its own section below rather than in the AA scorecard.

Measured against the actual AA criterion, both arms pass. The control's four nav
links sit 100.5px to 110px apart center to center and the treatment's five sit
82.5px to 131px apart, against a 24px requirement. Neither is close to the line.
A previous correction to this file claimed the treatment's five links were too
crowded to earn the exception. That was reasoning from the link count rather than
measuring the gaps, and the measurement does not support it.

The one real separation on the AA criteria is magnitude within 1.4.3. Both arms fail
the criterion, since a single failing pairing fails it, but the control fails it
twenty-eight times and the treatment once. That gap is large and it is genuine. It
is not a clean sheet.

Two findings outside the scored criteria are more useful than the scores.

**The skill changed how layers were named.** The treatment contains eight layers
named `accessible-button` and one named `accessible-input`. The control contains
zero layers with any semantic interactive name; its most common layer name is
`Frame`, ninety-eight times. Naming is not a WCAG criterion, but it is the
mechanism by which design intent survives handoff to code.

**Neither arm recorded alt text.** The control has twelve image-filled
rectangles, all named `Rectangle`. The treatment has eleven, ten named
`Rectangle` and one `hero-full-bleed-image`. Both fail the same way. This matches
study 5's build finding that the unguided baseline has moved on image handling,
and it means the toolkit should stop treating image naming in design as
differentiating value.

# 1.4.3 Text contrast

The decisive criterion.

| | Control | Treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Distinct pairings | 47 | 41 |
| Text layers | 118 | 130 |
| Failing pairings | **28** | **1** |

Large text was evaluated at the 3:1 threshold per the WCAG definition, 24px
regular or 18.66px bold and above.

## Control: 28 failures, one root cause

Every failure traces to a single decision. The control chose a rust accent,
`#c45b3e`, and used it for small bold labels, prices, and eyebrow text on four
different backgrounds. It clears 3:1 on all of them and clears 4.5:1 on none of
them.

Note the shape of it. The worst case is 3.59:1 and the best is 4.27:1. Nothing
is wildly wrong. The accent is 5% to 20% short of the threshold across the board,
which is the failure mode least likely to be caught by eye and most likely to
survive to production. The 4.27:1 cases in particular look correct in review.

Two of those rows deserve attention on their own. The four product prices are 18px
bold, which sits just under the 18.66px bold large-text boundary, so they need
4.5:1 rather than 3:1 and miss it. And the same rust at 48px passes comfortably
at the 3:1 large-text threshold, which is why the palette reads as working: the
big applications are fine and only the small ones fail.

Everything the control set in its neutral ramp passes, most with wide margin:
`#5c4a42` body text at 7.03:1 to 8.36:1, `#332019` at 14.43:1 to 15.43:1, and the
inverse `#faf7f2` on `#332019` at 14.43:1. The control did not have a general
contrast problem. It had one accent color it never rechecked at small sizes.

## A measurement error worth recording

My first pass scored the control as failing 1.4.11. Four menu cards contain a
"Reserve Boule" call to action in a 240 x 32 frame whose only stroke is
`#e7dfd5` at 1.32:1, which reads as a button with an unusably faint border.

Reading `individualStrokeWeights` showed the stroke is `{top: 1, right: 0,
bottom: 0, left: 0}`. It is a top rule above a text link, not a box around a
button. Text links are covered by 1.4.3, not 1.4.11, and that text measures
15.43:1. The finding was mine, not the control's.

This is the design-file analogue of the compositing error in the build study.
`strokeWeight` alone is not enough to tell a border from a divider, and any audit
script that ignores `individualStrokeWeights` will invent boundary failures.

**Both arms miss the house standard on the nav links,** and this is the honest

auto-layout row rather than as padded targets.

numbers above are read from the same node trees that were available when the claim
was made.

Neither arm meets the 44px house standard here, and in both files the room
already exists: the bars are 89px and 96px tall. Fourteen pixels of vertical
padding per link would resolve it without changing either layout.

This is now a confirmed gap in the skill rather than a one-off. Queued for the
next release.

# What a design file influences but neither arm recorded

These are decided during design and implemented in code. Both files settled them
visually and left no record, which means the intent does not survive handoff.

**Alt text. Both arms fail.** Control: twelve image-filled rectangles, all named
`Rectangle`, zero descriptive. Treatment: eleven, ten named `Rectangle` and one
`hero-full-bleed-image`, which is a role name rather than a description. Section 1
rule 5 is explicit that a filename or a generic name tells a customer nothing,
and `Rectangle` is the generic case.

A correction to the earlier treatment-only version of this audit, which credited
the treatment with the descriptive name "Baker hand-shaping our signature Country
Sourdough." That is a visible caption, a `TEXT` layer whose name defaults to its
own content. It is not attached to an image layer and is not alt text. The
treatment recorded no alt text either.

**Layer naming. The clearest difference in the file.**

| | Control | Treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nodes | 275 | 296 |
| Carrying a default name | 129, 47% | 117, 40% |
| Layers named `accessible-button` | 0 | 8 |
| Layers named `accessible-input` | 0 | 1 |
| Most common layer name | `Frame`, x98 | `Frame` |

The treatment named every interactive element for what it is. The control named
none of them. This does not map to a WCAG criterion and is not scored above, but
it is the single most transferable thing a design file can carry into code, and
it is the one place the skill visibly changed the output structure rather than
the output values.

**Heading levels. Neither arm recorded them.** Both imply hierarchy through type
size and state no levels. The control uses eighteen distinct font sizes against
the treatment's fifteen, topping out at 160px for a display wordmark and 80px for
the hero. A generator reading size alone would have more ambiguity to resolve in
the control, and both files contain display figures that are not headings: the
control's 48px "78%" and 40px "100%" hydration stats, the treatment's 36px "36h,"
"100%," and "Zero."

**Focus and reading order. Neither arm recorded them.** Layer order runs sensibly
top to bottom in both. Both have multi-column sections where a generator commonly
reads down one column then the other rather than across.

**Accessibility annotations.** Effectively zero in both arms. A keyword sweep for
alt, aria, heading level, focus, role, label, reading order, and decorative found
zero hits in the treatment and one incidental hit in the control, matching body
copy about delivery cancellation rather than an annotation.

**Structural parity.** Neither arm used a single component, instance, or shared
style. Auto layout coverage is similar, 135 of 140 frames in the treatment and
115 of 125 in the control. The skill did not make the agent build a system, and
it did not need to.

# Method

Both node trees pulled via the Figma REST API at full depth on 2026-08-14 and
scored by the same script in the same run. Contrast computed from each file's own
solid fill and stroke values using the WCAG relative luminance formula, with each
text layer resolved against its nearest opaque ancestor background. Fills below
95% opacity treated as transparent, which correctly identified the ghost buttons
in both arms as bordered rather than filled. Target sizes read from absolute
bounding boxes. Large-text thresholds applied per the WCAG definition of 24px
regular and 18.66px bold. Boundary versus divider distinguished by reading
`individualStrokeWeights`, not `strokeWeight`.

Scope limits, stated plainly:

**This scores three criteria, not twenty.** A static design file determines 3 of
the 55 WCAG 2.2 A and AA criteria, influences 14, and cannot affect 38. There is
nothing running to evaluate. A three criterion result, which at strict AA is a 1 of
3 tie, is not comparable in weight to the 8 of 16 versus 14 of 16 from the paired
Figma Make builds, and it should not be quoted as though it were.

**One page per arm, one prompt, one day.** No repeat runs. Single-sample results
on generative output show direction, not effect size.

**What this does test well.** The three criteria scored here are exactly the three
the ceiling analysis predicts a design artifact can determine. The control
confirms the prediction from the other side: it failed the criterion a design file
most directly controls, 1.4.3, twenty-eight times, and it left every criterion a
design file cannot reach in precisely the same unrecorded state as the treatment.
That is the ceiling being visible in both directions.

**What it does not test.** This measures a design file, not design. Design as a
practice sets intent, records decisions, and reviews them, none of which a node
tree can show. The unrecorded alt text, heading levels, and reading order in both
arms are the argument for design review, not against it. A file cannot annotate
itself.

# Scoring corrections

## First correction

This file originally reported **1 of 3 against 3 of 3** on the criteria a design
file determines. That was wrong twice, and both errors favored the toolkit.

**It scored a AAA-level bar under an AA label.** The toolkit requires 44 x 44 targets
as a house standard. WCAG 2.2 sets Level AA at
[24 x 24 with a spacing exception (SC 2.5.8)](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#target-size-minimum),
and 44 x 44 is
[SC 2.5.5, Level AAA](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#target-size-enhanced). Scoring
41px buttons as a 2.5.8 failure told readers the control missed a Level AA
requirement. It did not. It missed a stricter house standard that this project
recommends for good reasons, and the toolkit's own Section 3.5 has always described
it correctly.

**On the real AA criterion the control passes.** Reading this file's own measurement
tables against 24 x 24: the control's four nav links are 100.5px to 110px apart and
clear the spacing exception, so the control meets 2.5.8. The first correction also
claimed the treatment failed the same exception. It does not, and that claim is
retracted in the third correction below.

**And the treatment never had a clean sheet on 1.4.3.** One failing pairing fails a
criterion. The treatment has one, at 2.71:1. Reporting it as a pass applied binary
conformance to the control and magnitude to the treatment.

## How this was caught

Worth recording plainly, because it is the most useful thing in this file.

This audit was produced by an AI agent working from the node trees, and the agent
set the bar wrong in a way that favored the toolkit it was evaluating. It took the
toolkit's own 44 x 44 house standard, treated it as the requirement, filed it under
the Level AA criterion number, and then reported buttons that missed it as
accessibility failures. It never checked the direction of the spacing exception, which
is the thing that reverses the result.

To be precise about what it did not do, since a first reading of the tables above
suggests otherwise: no target at 48px was ever scored as a failure. Every 48px
treatment control passes both bars. The failing rows that contain a 48 are navigation
links measuring 48 to 73 pixels **wide** and 17 pixels **tall**, and a 17px height
misses both 44 and 24. Those tables now label the width and height explicitly,
because reading a width as a height is an easy mistake to make and it was made during
review of this correction.

None of that was a measurement error. Every dimension it recorded was correct. It
was a judgment error about which standard applied, and it ran in the direction of
the more impressive finding.

It was caught by a human asking one question: is 44 actually required, or is it just
the minimum we recommend. That question came from a reviewer with accessibility
domain knowledge reading a result that looked too clean. No automated check would
have produced it, because the arithmetic was internally consistent. The number was
right and the standard was wrong, and only someone who knows the difference between
Level AA and Level AAA can see that.

This is the practical version of a claim the toolkit makes in its scope section, that
these tools reduce the volume of obvious defects and do not remove the need for human
expertise. Here that applied to the evaluation itself. An AI graded its own
intervention generously and a person with the domain knowledge caught it.

If you are using AI to audit accessibility, this is the failure mode to watch. Not
bad measurements. Correct measurements scored against the wrong bar, presented with
enough internal consistency to look verified.

## Third correction, 2026-08-14

The correction above was itself wrong in two places, and its scorecard did not add
up. All three errors are below. This round came out of testing an unrelated thing:
a review skill was run against both design files, and checking its output against
the node trees meant re-measuring what this audit had already claimed.

**The treatment fails 1.4.11.** Its Subscribe button is `#a04e27` on `#2c2115` at
2.71:1, against a 3:1 requirement for a control boundary. This file recorded it at
5.05:1 in the 1.4.11 table while recording the same pair correctly at 2.71:1 in the
1.4.3 table twenty rows earlier. The contradiction sat in the open and survived the
first correction, which read both of those tables.

**The treatment passes 2.5.8.** The first correction claimed its five nav links were
too crowded to earn the spacing exception, and reversed the headline on that basis.
No gap was ever measured. The centers sit 82.5px to 131px apart against a 24px
requirement. The claim was inferred from the link count, which is the same category
of error as the one it was correcting: reasoning toward a plausible answer instead
of reading the number.

**The control's total was wrong arithmetic.** Its scorecard row read Fail, Pass,
Pass and totaled 1 of 3. Two passes is 2 of 3. The wrong total made the arms look
tied, which was the shape the surrounding prose had already committed to.

Corrected scorecard, strict AA:

| Criterion | Control | Treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1.4.3 Text contrast | Fail, 28 pairings | Fail, 1 pairing |
| 1.4.11 Non-text contrast | Pass | **Fail**, 2.71:1 |
| 2.5.8 Target size, 24 x 24 | Pass | Pass |
| **Total** | **2 of 3** | **1 of 3** |

**The toolkit arm scores below the control on the headline count.** It is a
three-criterion scorecard and it turns on one button, so it should not be read as
more than it is, but it should not be softened either. Two of the three scorings
this file has published favored the toolkit and both were wrong. The one that does
not favor it is the one that survived measurement.

The finding that has not moved through any of it: the control fails 1.4.3 twenty-eight
times and the treatment fails it once. Every scoring of this file, including the two
wrong ones, has produced that same twenty-eight-fold gap. It is the only number here
that was never in dispute, and it is the reason the study is worth keeping.

The measurements of what is actually in the two files have held up throughout. Every
dimension and every color pairing read from the node trees has survived
re-measurement. What failed three times was the layer on top: which bar applied,
which row a value belonged to, and whether the totals added up.
